# Should the cost model drive LX allocation? A design note

**Short answer: no.** The decision is real — worth up to 2.74× measured — but the compiler
already has a heuristic that gets it right, and the one case where a cost model beats that
heuristic is a corner case not worth building for. What the study did find is a free 18.2%
sitting in the default solver, and a model bug that has to be fixed regardless.

Every number comes from `research/findings_lx.md`, generated by `research/lx_choice.py`
against the recovered measurement database. Nothing has been confirmed on hardware.

---

## 1. The decision, and who makes it today

When several intermediates compete for a scratchpad too small to hold them all, which stay
resident? Two layers answer that, and **neither knows about time**
(full detail in `research/lx_allocator.md`):

**Eligibility** — a hard-coded 19-op allowlist decides which buffers may be considered at all.

**Placement** — one of five solvers, selected by `config.layout_solver`, decides which of
those actually fit and where. They are:

| solver | how it decides what to keep | has an objective? |
|---|---|---|
| **`greedy`** *(the default)* | Walks the program in order, placing each buffer when it is first used. Whoever arrives when LX is full loses. | **no** |
| `firstfit` | Sorts by `(lifetime − discount) / uses` ascending — short-lived, heavily-reused buffers first — then takes the first gap that fits. | a proxy |
| `bestfit` | Same ordering as `firstfit`; picks the *tightest* gap instead of the first. | a proxy |
| `cpsat` | 2-D packing solved by OR-Tools, minimising the HBM traffic that spilling adds: `Σ (reads + 1) · size` over spilled buffers. | **yes, in bytes** |
| `simulated_annealing` | Anneals over buffer orderings, scoring fragmentation. | fragmentation |

Only `cpsat` expresses "which buffer is worth more", and it expresses it in bytes. The
proposal under consideration would replace that byte objective with predicted microseconds.

## 2. The model cannot choose alone — it can only rank

The cost model charges nothing for LX traffic (a resident buffer simply drops out of the HBM
byte count) and knows nothing about how much LX exists. Asked to choose freely it answers
"keep everything", every time. So any design has to be:

> the allocator enumerates *feasible* allocations; the model *ranks* them.

That is the correct split rather than a limitation: capacity is a structural fact the
allocator already tracks with liveness intervals, and ranking is a performance judgement it
has no way to make.

## 3. The evidence

### 3.1 The stakes are real

LX residency is worth up to **2.74×** measured (`add6`, 2048×1024), growing with fused chain
depth. `add{N}_sep` — the same arithmetic in separate kernels, which cannot keep anything on
chip — sits at 1.01×, which identifies the mechanism.

### 3.2 The byte objective is not an approximation — it is exact

Both policies estimate the same thing and differ in one step. The byte objective counts the
traffic a spill adds, `(consumers + 1) × size`. The cost model takes that same traffic and
divides by the rate it moves at, `traffic ÷ bandwidth`.

So when every candidate moves at the same bandwidth, the two rank *identically* — dividing
every score by one constant cannot reorder anything. On the recorded corpus every op runs at
the default 150 GB/s, and `cpsat` is time-optimal on **36 of 37** contested bundles, worst
regret 0.6%. That is not luck; it is arithmetic.

### 3.3 The default solver is the actual problem

| solver | time-optimal on | worst regret |
|---|---:|---:|
| `cpsat` | 36/37 | +0.6% |
| **`greedy`** *(default)* | **21/37** | **+18.2%** |
| `firstfit` / `bestfit` | 21/37 | +18.2% |

`greedy` fails the same way every time. In a row-tiled softmax the intermediates are `b0`
(`amax`), `b1` (`sub`), `b2` (`exp`), `b3` (`sum`). `b1` and `b2` are byte-identical, but
`b2` feeds *two* consumers and is worth 1.7× more. Walking in program order, `greedy` reaches
`b1` first, takes it, and has no room left for `b2`.

**`firstfit`/`bestfit` do not fix this**, which is worth stating because it is the obvious
thing to try. Their ordering scores `b1` at `2/2.5 = 0.80` against `b2`'s `3/3.5 = 0.86`, so
they also take `b1` first. Only CP-SAT's `(reads + 1) · size` separates them.

### 3.4 A blocker: the model is not monotone in residency

On **14/37** contested bundles the model predicts that moving a buffer *into* LX makes the
kernel **slower**, by up to 2.4%. The cause is a branch discontinuity, not physics: spilling
a small broadcast operand makes its consumer a broadcast op, which flips the whole bundle
from the `bw_peak + α·min(R,W)` formula onto the per-op effective-bandwidth formula, and that
branch does not charge turnaround at all.

A ranker cannot be more trustworthy than the ordering it ranks by, so this blocks any
model-driven policy. It is worth fixing on its own account: two programs differing only in
whether an operand is broadcast are currently priced by different formulas with a step
between them.

## 4. Checked and ruled out: the corner case where a cost model wins

For completeness, since it was the original hypothesis. The byte objective and the model can
only disagree when candidates move at different bandwidths. The model's rates span 3.75×
(150 GB/s default down to 40 for a penalised `transpose_outer`, 44 for `cat0`), and the
shape-dependent ones widen with tensor size — `cat0` is 89 GB/s at `[2048,512]` and hits its
44 GB/s floor at the Granite MLP shape. Perturbing a real bundle by marking one op with each
access pattern, the byte objective's regret reaches **36.6%** when a `stick_scatter` buffer
is in contention, and the flat patterns never cost more than 1.3%.

So the effect is real and the trigger is cheap to test for (does any LX-eligible candidate
carry `stick_scatter` or a penalised `transpose_outer`?). **It is still not worth building
for**: it needs a corner case to fire, it was shown by perturbing recorded features rather
than on any real program, and its ceiling is a fraction of what §5 and §6 offer. Recorded as
a finding, not a direction.

## 5. Recommended, and shippable now

1. **Change the default `layout_solver` from `greedy` to `cpsat`.** 21/37 → 36/37, worst
   regret 18.2% → 0.6%. One config line, no cost model. Costs an OR-Tools dependency and a
   600 s solve limit; `select_allocator` already degrades to `greedy` with a warning when
   OR-Tools is absent. *If `cpsat` is unacceptable, note that neither `firstfit` nor
   `bestfit` is a substitute — both measure identically to `greedy`.*
2. **Emit a WARNING when a buffer is denied LX for want of room.** Today the compiler's most
   consequential allocation event is invisible above DEBUG.
3. **Rename `lx_spill_cap_bytes`.** It reads as the LX capacity and differs from the real
   budget by 3.1×, but setting it to the true value measurably degrades softmax — it is a
   fitted derate knee. The name invites a "fix" that makes the model worse.
4. **Fix the monotonicity break in §3.4**, so predicted time never falls when residency rises.

Items 1–3 are independent of everything else here.

## 6. What is not solved, and where to go instead

The honest summary of this study is that **LX residency is a solved problem with a broken
default**. The interesting work is elsewhere, and the magnitudes say where:

| | worth | who gets it today |
|---|---|---|
| Choosing LX residency well | up to 2.74× | `cpsat` already does |
| Fixing the default solver | 18.2% | nobody — free, item 1 above |
| The §4 corner case | 36.6%, corner case, perturbed features | the direction this note rejects |
| Layout-shuffle elimination on AIU (DNNDaSher, per the review) | **2.3× average end-to-end** | published, adjacent to `insert_restickify` |

Three directions that are not incremental:

**Tile size, where there is no incumbent at all.** Tile counts come from user `spyre_hint`s —
every one in `profile_ops.py`, and the five-deep stack inside the SDPA decomposition, was
written by a human who had to know the answer. A cost model here does not beat a heuristic by
a few percent; it replaces *the user must know* with *the compiler decides*. The model is
already good enough to try: over the 26 coarse-tiling ladders with data, regret was 0% on 17
of them, mean 3.9%. This is the one decision where the model is the only available instrument.

**Whether capacity is even the binding constraint.** The literature review turned up that
tt-mlir found spills on their hardware are *constraint-driven, not memory-driven* — 40–94% of
on-chip memory still free when spills fired. If Spyre behaves the same way, then capacity,
contested bundles and allocation ranking are all the wrong axis, and layout legality is the
real one. Cheap to test, and it either kills or validates a whole line of work.

**Layout conversion, where IBM's own published numbers point.** DNNDaSher reports 2.3×
average end-to-end on AIU purely from eliminating and coarsening layout shuffles, and
onnx-mlir's NNPA cost model — the direct ancestor of this one — has its worst fits on exactly
`Stick` (r² 0.691) and `Unstick` (0.429). The hardest thing to model on this hardware is also
the most valuable thing to optimise.
